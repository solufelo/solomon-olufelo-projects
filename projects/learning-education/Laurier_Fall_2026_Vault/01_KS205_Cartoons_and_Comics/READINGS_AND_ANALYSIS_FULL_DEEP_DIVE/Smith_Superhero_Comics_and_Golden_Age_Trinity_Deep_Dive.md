## 1. Defining the Superhero: Peter Coogan’s Tripartite Formula

While the concept of superhuman beings traces back through world mythology (Gilgamesh, Hercules, Samson, Sun Wukong), the modern **superhero** is a distinct 20th-century cultural and generic invention codified in American comic books.

Scholars frequently debated what qualifies a character as a superhero (e.g., does Batman qualify without metahuman powers?). Media scholar **Peter Coogan (2006)** provided the definitive scholarly definition:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                 PETER COOGAN'S DEFINITION OF THE SUPERHERO                  │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ "A heroic character with a selfless, pro-social MISSION;                    │
│  with extraordinary POWERS (abilities, advanced technology, or highly       │
│  developed physical, mental, or mystical skills);                           │
│  who has a superhero IDENTITY embodied in a codename and iconic costume,    │
│  which typically expresses his biography, character, powers, or origin;     │
│  and who is generically distinct from characters of related genres."        │
│                                           — Peter Coogan (2006: 30)         │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

```
                     ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
                     │         COOGAN'S TRIPARTITE TRIAD       │
                     └─────────────────────────────────────────┘
                                          │
         ┌────────────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────┐
         ▼                                ▼                                ▼
      MISSION                          POWERS                          IDENTITY
Selfless, pro-social,           Extraordinary abilities,        Iconic costume & codename;
ongoing commitment to help      super-human physiology, or      Externalizes character origin;
humanity & conquer evil.        improbable peak training.       Dual/secret civilian identity.
```

### Additional Generic Conventions (Coogan 2013):
Beyond Mission, Powers, and Identity, the superhero genre requires:
1. **Supervillains**: Antagonists of equal or greater power whose twisted mirror motivations justify the superhero's extreme actions.
2. **Urban Settings**: Vast metropolises (Metropolis, Gotham City, New York) whose skyscrapers and dense crowds showcase kinetic verticality and widespread civilian vulnerability.
3. **Helpful Authority Figures**: Sympathetic police commissioners (Jim Gordon) or journalists (Lois Lane) who validate the vigilante outside the slow machinery of the state.

## 2. Walter Wink & The "Myth of Redemptive Violence"

Matthew J. Smith and Duncan et al. (2015) emphasize that the superhero genre is structurally anchored in **violence**: superheroes punch, blast, and batter evil into submission.

Theologian **Walter Wink (1998)** coined the term **The Myth of Redemptive Violence** to describe the underlying ideology of modern adventure storytelling:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                  THE MYTH OF REDEMPTIVE VIOLENCE (WINK 1998)                │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 1. The world is divided into pure Good and irredeemable Evil.               │
│ 2. Evil is aggressive, irrational, and cannot be reasoned with.             │
│ 3. Peace, order, and justice CANNOT be restored through diplomacy,          │
│    institutional reform, or empathy.                                        │
│ 4. Order can ONLY be restored when the righteous hero applies overwhelming, │
│    superior physical violence to obliterate the antagonist.                 │
│ 5. Therefore, violence is redemptive, moral, and heroic.                    │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### The Superhero Paradox:
* The superhero operates as an extra-legal vigilante who bypasses democratic due process.
* While the hero fights to uphold the social order, they do so by demonstrating that the legal and judicial systems of society are completely powerless to protect its citizens without an authoritarian protector.

### A. Superman: The Great Depression Folk Hero & Immigrant Allegory
* **Historical Context**: Created by two young Jewish high school graduates from Cleveland, Ohio during the depths of the Great Depression and the rise of fascism in Europe.
* **The Original Social Crusader**:
  - In *Action Comics #1* (1938), Superman did not fight alien warlords; he fought **corrupt slumlords, exploitative factory owners, crooked politicians, and domestic wife-beaters**.
  - He pulled down uninspected tenements, forced a war-profiteering lobbyist to enlist in the infantry, and threw a crooked judge through a wall. Superman was a populist, New Deal social avenger defending the working class.
* **The Immigrant Narrative**: Kal-El is the ultimate refugee—sent away from an exploding homeland (symbolizing Eastern European Jewish diaspora fleeing persecution), landing in the American heartland, adopting an Anglo-Saxon name (Clark Kent), and assimilating while concealing his extraordinary foreign heritage.

---

### B. Batman: The Pulp Noir Detective & Gothic Trauma
* **Historical Context**: Created by Bob Kane and writer Bill Finger in response to National's demand for another hit character following Superman’s explosive success.
* **Pulp Roots**: Heavy influence from pulp magazine heroes: *The Shadow* (cloaked vigilante laughing at criminals), *The Bat Whispers* (1930 film), and *Zorro* (wealthy playboy concealing secret cave).
* **The No-Powers Exception**:
  - Bruce Wayne possesses **no metahuman genetic powers**.
  - His "powers" are economic capital (billions of inherited wealth), technological superiority (the Batmobile, Batarangs, grapple guns), forensic intellect (the World's Greatest Detective), and peak human athletic mastery.
* **Psychological Foundation**: Unlike Superman’s benevolent origin, Batman is born of raw childhood trauma (witnessing the cold-blooded murder of Thomas and Martha Wayne in Crime Alley). His war on crime is a compulsive, gothic crusade to impose absolute order on an irrational, corrupt urban landscape (Gotham).

---

### C. Wonder Woman: Feminist Allegory & The Truth of Love
* **Historical Context**: Created by Harvard-trained psychologist **William Moulton Marston** (who also invented the systolic blood-pressure lie-detector test) and artist **H.G. Peter**.
* **Marston’s Psychological Agenda**:
  - Marston explicitly created Wonder Woman as a psychological antidote to the hyper-masculine, bloodthirsty violence of Superman and Batman:
    > *"Wonder Woman is psychological propaganda for the new type of woman who should, I believe, rule the world... Frankly, Wonder Woman is psychological propaganda for female values: love, empathy, and submission to loving authority."*  
    > — William Moulton Marston
* **Subverting the Generic Conventions**:
  - **The Lasso of Truth**: Instead of guns or brute fists, Diana’s primary weapon is the golden lasso, compelling enemies to surrender and confront the unvarnished psychological truth of their actions.
  - **The Theme of Bondage & Liberation**: Diana frequently submits to being bound or chained by male captors, only to summon inner spiritual strength and break free, symbolizing female emancipation from patriarchal confinement.

## 4. Historical Waves of the Comic Book Medium

Smith outlines the broader historical arc of American comic books:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐

├────────────────────┼───────────┼────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 1. The Golden Age  │ 1938–1956 │ Birth of superheroes; WW2 patriotism;      │
│                    │           │ newsstand boom; massive circulations.      │

```

### The Post-War Moral Panic & The Comics Code (1954):
* In 1954, German-American psychiatrist **Dr. Fredric Wertham** published *Seduction of the Innocent*, claiming comic books caused juvenile delinquency, violence, homosexuality (claiming Batman and Robin were a gay couple), and sexual deviance.
* A U.S. Senate Subcommittee held televised hearings. To avoid federal censorship, publishers formed the **Comics Magazine Association of America (CMAA)** and created the strict **Comics Code Authority (CCA)** seal, banning horror, gore, sympathetic criminals, and corruption of authority figures for decades.

## 5. High-Yield Examination Flashcards

> **Q1: What are Peter Coogan’s three core components of a superhero?**  
> **A**: Mission (selfless, pro-social, ongoing), Powers (extraordinary abilities, technology, or peak physical/mental training), and Identity (iconic costume and codename expressing character origin, usually with a secret identity).

> **Q2: Define Walter Wink’s "Myth of Redemptive Violence."**  
> **A**: The ideological belief that the world is locked in a struggle between pure good and absolute evil, where evil cannot be reformed and justice can only be restored through the hero applying overwhelming, superior physical violence.

> **Q3: How did Superman’s early 1938 appearances reflect the socio-economic climate of the Great Depression?**  
> **A**: In early issues of *Action Comics*, Superman acted as a New Deal populist avenger who attacked systemic social ills—confronting exploitative landlords, corrupt politicians, price-gouging factory owners, and wife-beaters—rather than alien supervillains.

> **Q4: Who created Wonder Woman, and what was his psychological rationale?**  
> **A**: Psychologist William Moulton Marston (with artist H.G. Peter). He created Wonder Woman as feminist propaganda to counter masculine violence, promoting love, empathy, and female leadership through symbols like the Lasso of Truth.

> **Q5: What was the impact of Dr. Fredric Wertham’s *Seduction of the Innocent* (1954)?**  
> **A**: It sparked a national moral panic accusing comics of causing juvenile delinquency, leading to Senate hearings and the creation of the self-censoring Comics Code Authority (CCA), which sanitized the medium for decades.
