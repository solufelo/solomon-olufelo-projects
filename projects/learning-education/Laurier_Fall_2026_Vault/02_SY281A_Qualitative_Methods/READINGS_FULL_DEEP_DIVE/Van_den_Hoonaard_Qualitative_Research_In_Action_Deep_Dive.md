## 1. The Core Qualitative Philosophy: Inductive vs. Deductive Logic

Deborah van den Hoonaard grounds Canadian qualitative inquiry in the radical contrast between the deductive, hypothesis-testing approach of positivist quantitative sociology and the inductive, exploratory approach of qualitative sociology.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│          DEDUCTIVE (QUANTITATIVE)       VS.      INDUCTIVE (QUALITATIVE)     │
├─────────────────────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────┤
│ Starts with Theory & Hypotheses         │ Starts with Empirical Observation │
│ "Top-Down" testing of existing models   │ "Bottom-Up" generation of theory  │
│ Rigid, fixed pre-determined design      │ Emergent, flexible, iterative     │
│ Variables, numerical metrics, scales    │ Words, meanings, bodily practices │
│ Generalizability across large samples   │ Contextual depth & transferability│
│ Researcher is detached & neutral        │ Researcher is reflexive instrument│
└─────────────────────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Key Methodological Foundations:
1. **Emergent Design**: Qualitative research cannot be completely planned in advance behind an office desk. As the researcher enters the field, talks with participants, and observes interactions, the design *emerges* and shifts. Research questions are refined dynamically based on what participants emphasize.
2. **Iterative Process**: Data collection and data analysis are **not sequential**; they occur simultaneously. You collect a few interviews, analyze them, write memos, identify emerging gaps or themes, and then adjust your subsequent interview questions or sampling strategy accordingly.
3. **Sensitizing Concepts (Herbert Blumer)**:
   - While quantitative researchers use *definitive concepts* (rigid operational definitions, e.g., "poverty = income under \$25,000"), qualitative researchers use **sensitizing concepts**.
   - A sensitizing concept does not provide a fixed prescription of what to see; it simply gives the researcher a general sense of reference and guidance in approaching empirical instances (*"a place to start looking, not a final conclusion"*). Examples: "stigma," "identity work," "liminality."

## 2. Asking Qualitative Research Questions & Identifying Goals

### What Makes a Strong Qualitative Research Question?
* **Open-Ended & Non-Directional**: Qualitative questions never test whether Variable X causes Variable Y. They do not predict an outcome.
* **Process-Oriented**: Focus on *how* people experience, negotiate, interpret, and make sense of their lives.
* **Avoid Quantitative Pitfalls**:
  - ❌ *Poor*: "Does social media cause depression in first-year university students?" (Quantitative/causal hypothesis).
  - ✅ *Strong*: "How do first-year university students describe the role of social media in negotiating feelings of loneliness and belonging during their transition to campus life?"

## 3. Generic Social Processes (GSPs): Robert Prus (1996)

One of the most important conceptual frameworks highlighted by Van den Hoonaard (and frequently tested in SY 281) is **Robert Prus’s Generic Social Processes (GSPs)**. 

GSPs are trans-situational, fundamental forms of human social interaction that occur across widely diverse empirical settings. By identifying GSPs, a qualitative researcher can connect a highly specific study (e.g., amateur skateboarders) to broader sociological theory (e.g., subcultural identity achievement).

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                 ROBERT PRUS'S GENERIC SOCIAL PROCESSES (GSPs)               │
├─────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ GSP Category            │ Concrete Sub-Processes & Sociological Meaning     │
├─────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 1. Acquiring            │ Learning the jargon, adopting cultural viewpoints,│
│    Perspectives         │ internalizing group norms, undergoing socialization│
├─────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 2. Achieving            │ Presenting oneself, establishing credibility,     │
│    Identity             │ managing stigma, claiming membership, role-taking │
├─────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 3. Being                │ Entering groups, maintaining commitment, drifting,│
│    Involved             │ resisting, burning out, disengaging, exiting       │
├─────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 4. Performing           │ Developing technical competence, coordinating with│
│    Activity             │ peers, handling routine obstacles, improvising    │
├─────────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 5. Experiencing         │ Building trust, navigating conflict, negotiating  │
│    Relationships        │ hierarchy, managing intimacy, facing betrayal     │
└─────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

> **Why GSPs Matter for Exams & Labs**: Whenever you analyze interview transcripts or observational field notes in SY 281, linking specific participant actions to a GSP (e.g., *"Here the participant is engaging in the GSP of 'Achieving Identity' through stigma management"*) immediately elevates your answer to top-tier academic rigor.

## 4. Canadian Research Ethics: The TCPS 2 Framework

In Canada, all university-based research involving human participants is legally and ethically governed by the **Tri-Council Policy Statement: Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans (TCPS 2)**, funded by Canada's three federal research agencies:
1. **CIHR** (Canadian Institutes of Health Research)
2. **NSERC** (Natural Sciences and Engineering Research Council)
3. **SSHRC** (Social Sciences and Humanities Research Council)

```
                     ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
                     │          TCPS 2 CORE PRINCIPLES         │
                     └─────────────────────────────────────────┘
                                          │
         ┌────────────────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────┐
         ▼                                ▼                                ▼
RESPECT FOR PERSONS              CONCERN FOR WELFARE                    JUSTICE
Autonomy, dignity, free,        Physical, mental, social,       Fairness, equity, no
informed, ongoing consent,      and spiritual well-being;       exploitation; equitable
protection of vulnerable.       Minimizing risks vs benefits.   distribution of burdens.
```

### The Three Core Ethical Principles Dissected:

### 1. Respect for Persons
* **Autonomy**: Human beings have intrinsic moral worth and must be treated as autonomous agents capable of making their own decisions.
* **Informed Consent**:
  - Must be **free** (no coercion, undue influence, or financial intimidation).
  - Must be **informed** (participants understand the purpose, methods, foreseeable risks, potential benefits, and how data will be stored/used).
  - Must be **ongoing** (consent is not a one-time signature; participants have the continuous right to withdraw at any time without penalty).
* **Protection of Vulnerable Populations**: Individuals with diminished autonomy (children, prisoners, persons with cognitive impairments, economically precarious workers) require heightened procedural protections.

### 2. Concern for Welfare
* Welfare encompasses total quality of life: physical health, psychological well-being, social reputation, economic security, and cultural/spiritual integrity.
* **Risk vs. Benefit Analysis**: Foreseeable risks must never outweigh anticipated benefits.
* **The "Minimal Risk" Standard**: Research is classified as minimal risk if the probability and magnitude of possible harm are no greater than those encountered by participants in those aspects of their everyday life relevant to the research.

### 3. Justice
* **Fairness & Equity**: No single segment of the population should bear an unfair share of the burdens of research, nor should any group be unfairly excluded from its benefits.
* Historical exploitation (e.g., medical experimentation on marginalized groups) is redressed through strict inclusion/exclusion criteria.

## 5. TCPS 2 Chapter 9: Research Involving First Nations, Inuit, & Métis Peoples

Chapter 9 is one of the most critical sections of the TCPS 2, responding directly to centuries of colonial research exploitation (where non-Indigenous academics helicoptered into reserves, extracted stories and blood samples, published books, and left communities unchanged or harmed).

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                  THE OCAP® PRINCIPLES (FIRST NATIONS ETHICS)                │
├───────────────────┬─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ OWNERSHIP         │ The community owns its cultural knowledge, data, and    │
│                   │ collective intellectual property.                       │
├───────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ CONTROL           │ Indigenous peoples have the right to control all stages │
│                   │ of research: conception, methodology, analysis, sharing.│
├───────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ ACCESS            │ Indigenous communities must have full access to their   │
│                   │ own data and manage who outside accesses it.            │
├───────────────────┼─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ POSSESSION        │ Physical/digital storage of data must remain within the │
│                   │ community’s physical or jurisdictional stewardship.     │
└───────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### Key Mandates of Chapter 9:
* **Community Engagement**: Research cannot proceed merely through individual participant consent; researchers must engage the community governing authorities and elders *prior* to entering the field.
* **Collaborative Research Agreements**: Formal agreements detailing mutual expectations, data management, and benefit sharing.
* **Recognition of Indigenous Worldviews**: Validates oral histories, sacred knowledge protocols, and communal property concepts.

## 6. The Research Ethics Board (REB) & Operational Mechanics

* **Institutional Role**: Every Canadian university maintains an independent **Research Ethics Board (REB)** that reviews all faculty and student research proposals before any participant contact occurs.
* **Anonymity vs. Confidentiality**:
  - **Anonymity**: The researcher *never knows* the identity of the participant (e.g., anonymous online survey with no IP logging). Rarely possible in qualitative interviews.
  - **Confidentiality**: The researcher *knows* who the participant is, but actively protects that identity from the public through pseudonyms, altering identifying details, and storing audio recordings on encrypted drives.
* **Deception in Research**: Allowed *only* when indispensable to the study, minimal risk, justified by potential benefits, and followed immediately by a comprehensive **debriefing**.

## 7. Qualitative Data Analysis: Memos, Coding, and Grounded Theory

Van den Hoonaard outlines the classic stages of analyzing qualitative transcripts and field observations:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                 THE QUALITATIVE CODING PIPELINE                             │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ 1. Transcribing & Immersion (Listening, reading repeatedly, memo-writing)   │
│                                    │                                        │
│                                    ▼                                        │
│ 2. Open / Initial Coding (Line-by-line fracture of text; descriptive codes) │
│                                    │                                        │
│                                    ▼                                        │
│ 3. Axial / Focused Coding (Clustering codes into conceptual categories)     │
│                                    │                                        │
│                                    ▼                                        │
│ 4. Selective / Theoretical Coding (Connecting categories to GSPs & Theory)  │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

1. **Memoing**: Theoretical field notes written by the researcher to capture fleeting insights, analytical connections, emotional reactions, and emerging hypotheses during the research journey.
2. **Open Coding**: Close, line-by-line reading where codes stay close to the data, often using **In Vivo Codes** (the exact words spoken by participants, preserving their unique vernacular).
3. **Focused / Axial Coding**: Identifying the most frequent or significant initial codes and organizing them into broader conceptual families.
4. **Saturation**: The point in data collection and analysis where no new concepts, dimensions, or properties are emerging from additional interviews or observations.

## 8. High-Yield Examination & Lab Flashcards

> **Q1: Distinguish between Inductive and Deductive research.**  
> **A**: Deductive research begins with existing theory and tests predetermined hypotheses through quantitative measurement. Inductive research begins with empirical observations and lived experiences, generating conceptual insights and theory from the ground up.

> **Q2: What is a "Sensitizing Concept" (Herbert Blumer)?**  
> **A**: A concept that provides general guidance and direction for where to look in empirical research, rather than a rigid, definitive operational measurement.

> **Q3: What are the three core ethical principles of the TCPS 2?**  
> **A**: Respect for Persons (autonomy, free/informed/ongoing consent), Concern for Welfare (physical, social, and psychological well-being; risk minimization), and Justice (fairness, equity, non-exploitation).

> **Q4: What do the letters in OCAP® stand for, and what do they govern?**  
> **A**: Ownership, Control, Access, and Possession. They govern data sovereignty and ethical research conduct involving First Nations communities in Canada.

> **Q5: List four of Robert Prus’s Generic Social Processes (GSPs).**  
> **A**: Acquiring perspectives, Achieving identity, Being involved, Performing activity, and Experiencing relationships.
